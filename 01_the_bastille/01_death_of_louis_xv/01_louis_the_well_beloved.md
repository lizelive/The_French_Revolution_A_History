# Louis the Well-Beloved

Here's a fun fact: Louis XV was nicknamed "The Well-Beloved" (*le Bien-Aimé*). And holy crap, did he NOT deserve it by the end.

Back in 1744, when the King got sick in Metz, Paris absolutely *lost its mind*. People were sobbing in churches, priests couldn't get through their prayers without breaking down, and the whole city acted like someone had kicked their collective puppy. "The Well-Beloved!" they cried. What a guy! What a king!

Fast forward thirty years to 1774. Same King, different vibe. Now he's sick again, hauled back from his pleasure palace at Trianon to Versailles, and does anyone care? NOPE. The churches are quiet. Nobody's crying. The only prayers being said are by priests getting paid by the hour—and those guys would pray for literally anything if the money was right.

People are literally placing bets on when he'll die. Some are "expressing themselves loudly in the streets" (which is 18th-century French for "talking massive amounts of trash"). Meanwhile, the May sun keeps shining, people keep doing their thing, and nobody gives a damn about Old Louis sweating it out in his royal bed.

Who IS praying, though? Madame du Barry—the King's mistress. She'd better pray, because if Louis kicks the bucket, she's DONE. Same goes for her buddy the Duke d'Aiguillon, and Chancellor Maupeou. These three formed what was basically a corrupt power triangle, all propped up by the King's... let's call it "affection" for Madame du Barry.

Here's the tea on D'Aiguillon: This guy—a grand-nephew of the famous Cardinal Richelieu—had been disgraced and accused of corruption. He was basically canceled, about to slink off to his country estates to hunt deer and feel sorry for himself. But then! In 1770, a young soldier named Dumouriez saw King Louis literally bowing to Madame du Barry's carriage. The KING. Bowing to his MISTRESS. In front of the ARMY.

That's when D'Aiguillon saw his ticket back. The previous chief minister, Choiseul, had refused to acknowledge du Barry (he had standards, apparently). Big mistake. Du Barry pouted and cried until Louis—whom she called "France" like he was her personal servant—fired Choiseul. His chin was literally trembling as he did it, but he did it anyway.

So D'Aiguillon rose to power, along with Maupeou (who exiled anyone who disagreed with him to remote mountaintops) and Abbé Terray (a corrupt finance minister who paid only 8 pence on the shilling—basically ancient French fraud). Together, they built what we might call "du Barry-land": a corrupt fantasy world where the Chancellor played party games with the royal mistress and everyone pretended everything was fine.

"My Chancellor is a scoundrel," Louis reportedly said, "but I cannot do without him."

Cool. Cool cool cool.

But here's the thing about fantasy worlds: they collapse fast. If Louis dies—or even gets scared enough about dying—the whole house of cards comes down. This has happened before. When Louis got sick at Metz in 1744, his previous mistress had to flee. When a guy named Damiens stabbed him (not fatally), Madame de Pompadour had to pack her bags. Religious guilt is a hell of a drug.

Now the doctors are looking worried. They're privately asking: "Did the King actually have smallpox before, or was that a misdiagnosis?" Uh oh. If it's the real deal now, things are about to get very, very real.

Meanwhile, what about regular French people? Ha! They're not praying for Louis—if anything, they're praying *against* him. This is a France that's been crushed by military defeats (see: Rossbach in 1757, where Frederick the Great absolutely demolished the French army), corrupt finances, and the arbitrary imprisonment of over 60,000 people via *lettres de cachet* (basically, warrants that let the King jail anyone without trial).

The starving peasants trudging through the countryside, the millions grinding away in workshops, the desperate souls packed eight to a bed in charity hospitals—to them, the King is just "that guy who makes bread more expensive." When they hear he's sick, their response is basically: "Hope he dies."

And that, my friends, is France in 1774. The only interesting question left: Will he die?

*Spoiler: Yes.*
