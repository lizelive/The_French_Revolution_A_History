# Dishonored Bills: When the Credit Card Gets Declined

So France is a pressure cooker with multiple cracks in the lid. The question isn't IF it's going to explode, but WHERE. Which crack will give way first?

## Finding the Weak Spot

Every society has pressure valves—institutions where discontent can vent safely (or at least, controllably). Even Constantinople has them: you can tell how angry the population is by counting the fires and the hanged bakers.

France's main pressure valve? **The Parlement of Paris.**

Quick primer: The "Parlement" wasn't a real parliament like England's—it couldn't make laws. It was a high court, full of judges who had *bought* their positions (yes, that's how it worked). Their main power was that they had to register royal edicts before those edicts became law. If they refused to register an edict, things got complicated.

Louis XIV had bullied the Parlement into submission. But Louis XVI? Not so intimidating.

## Why the Judges Might Matter

Here's the thing about these Parlement judges: they're not living in a vacuum. They go to the same salons as the philosophes. They read Rousseau. They hear the same gossip. Their kids are all hopped up on Enlightenment ideas.

Plus, these guys have GRIEVANCES. The court calls them "Noblesse of the Robe"—lesser nobility than the real aristocrats. They're looked down upon even though they're rich and powerful. They'd LOVE a chance to push back against the Crown.

The Parlement is a sleeping dragon. And it's about to wake up.

## Meanwhile, Back at Versailles

Old Maurepas finally died in 1781. "Never more shall I hear his step overhead," said Louis XVI sadly. No more witty deflections of hard questions. No more kicking the can down the road.

Now it's just Louis and the boring, methodical Vergennes running things—a guy with all the imagination of a mid-level accountant. And Marie Antoinette, who has strong opinions and quick impulses but not much depth.

The Œil-de-Bœuf (the main waiting room at Versailles where courtiers gathered) is GLOOMY. The Horn of Plenty has run dry. Previous ministers suppressed hundreds of cushy court positions to save money, which means hundreds of angry aristocrats with grudges. Military reforms meant even more changes that offended the old guard.

"There was such a *tristesse* about Court," one observer noted, "as made it quite dispiriting to look upon."

## The Monster in the Room: BANKRUPTCY

Here's the ugly truth nobody wants to face: France is going BROKE.

Controller after Controller has tried to fix the finances and failed:
- Necker (the Swiss banker) did his best but eventually got pushed out
- Joly de Fleury proposed loans that nobody wanted to buy and taxes that made everyone angry
- D'Ormesson lasted only months before the King bought a palace without telling him, which he (correctly) interpreted as "you're fired"

Every finance minister hits the same wall: **The Deficit**. Revenue minus Expenses equals NEGATIVE NUMBERS. And no matter how clever you are, negative numbers eventually catch up with you.

## A Philosophical Interlude on Bankruptcy

Here's a dark truth: **Bankruptcy is nature's way of punishing lies.**

When you live beyond your means, when your promises exceed your ability to deliver, when your institutions are built on fiction rather than fact—eventually the bill comes due. And the answer comes back: "Insufficient funds."

The tragedy is that the ORIGINAL liars rarely pay the price. Lies get passed along, like a hot potato, until they land on whoever can't pass them further. Usually that's the poor—the ones at the bottom, who "with spade and mattock" actually touch reality every day.

But here's the justice: when the poor suffer, the pain works its way back UP. Eventually even dukes and kings feel it. Even Louis XVI has to have awkward fights with nobles about cutting their positions.

"We got into a real quarrel," Louis said about one such confrontation. "But if he had even struck me, I could not have blamed him."

That's how tense things have gotten.

## The Day of Reckoning

If France had unlimited money, the lies could continue indefinitely. But the money is running out. And when the money runs out, everyone suddenly notices that the emperor has no clothes.

Honor to Bankruptcy! Cruel in detail, but righteous overall. The great destroyer of comfortable delusions. The leveler that eventually sweeps away every falsehood, no matter how high it built itself.

France is about to learn this lesson firsthand.

Enter: a new Controller-General, armed with charm, confidence, and a complete inability to understand that you can't borrow your way out of a debt crisis.

His name is Calonne.

Get ready.
