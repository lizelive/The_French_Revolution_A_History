# Loménie's Thunderbolts: The Royal Smackdown

August 1787. The Parlement of Paris has refused to register Brienne's tax edicts. Time for the nuclear option: the *lit de justice*.

## What's a Lit de Justice?

Literally "bed of justice"—a special session where the King appears in person at the Parlement and COMMANDS them to register whatever he wants. It's the constitutional equivalent of a parent saying "because I SAID so."

The theory: The Parlement's authority derives from the King. When the King is physically present, their separate authority doesn't exist. They're just his voice. So whatever he orders, they MUST do.

The practice: It's humiliating. It makes the King look tyrannical. And everyone knows the judges only complied because they were forced.

But Brienne was out of options.

## The Royal Session

On August 6, 1787, Louis XVI appeared at the Parlement.

He wasn't good at this. Louis XVI was shy, awkward, more comfortable in his locksmithing workshop than giving speeches. He read his prepared remarks in a flat monotone. Marie Antoinette waited anxiously at Versailles, watching for his return.

The edicts were registered "by express command of His Majesty." The Parlement had no choice.

When Louis's carriage returned to Versailles, the Queen's brother-in-law clapped from afar—sign that it had worked!

## The Backlash

The next day, the Parlement met again. And they did something unprecedented: they declared the registration NULL AND VOID.

Their argument: A forced registration isn't real. The King can't make us do things that violate the fundamental laws of the kingdom. Those fundamental laws require that new taxes be approved by the nation.

This was revolutionary logic. They were saying the King's power had LIMITS. There were things even a *lit de justice* couldn't accomplish.

## Escalation

Brienne was furious. This was open rebellion!

His response: EXILE. The entire Parlement of Paris was ordered to relocate to Troyes, a provincial town far from the capital. Stay there and think about what you've done.

(This was a standard royal punishment for disobedient Parlements. Louis XV had done it. Louis XIV had done it. It usually worked—eventually the judges got bored in the provinces and came crawling back.)

But this time was different.

## The Court of Public Opinion

Paris went WILD with support for the exiled judges.

Pamphlets praised them as heroes of liberty. Crowds cheered as they departed. The contrast was stark: humble magistrates standing up for the rights of the nation, while the luxury-loving court tried to squeeze more taxes from the suffering people.

Never mind that those "humble magistrates" were wealthy nobles who'd bought their positions and were mainly trying to avoid paying taxes themselves. The narrative was set: Parlement = good, Court = bad.

Even other Parlements around France (there were several regional ones) started issuing supportive declarations. The crisis was spreading.

## The Fundamental Questions

What ARE the "fundamental laws" of France? Does the King have unlimited power, or are there constitutional limits? Who gets to interpret those limits?

These questions had always been fuzzy. French absolutism had worked by keeping them fuzzy. As long as everyone kind of went along, the system functioned.

But now people were asking the questions out loud. And every answer threatened someone.

If the King has unlimited power → the privileged classes lose their protections.
If the King's power is limited → who does the limiting? The Parlement? The States-General? The PEOPLE?

Brienne had stumbled into a constitutional crisis that went way beyond tax policy.

And he had absolutely no idea how to get out of it.

## The Stalemate

The Parlement sat in Troyes, refusing to budge.
The treasury sat empty, getting emptier.
The court sat at Versailles, running out of options.
Public opinion sat firmly against the government.

Something would have to give.

Would the Parlement surrender? Would the King surrender? Or would France have to find an entirely new way to settle things?
