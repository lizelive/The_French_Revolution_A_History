# Loménie's Death-Throes: The Final Collapse

Summer 1788. Brienne's government is dying. The only question is how ugly the end will be.

## The Money Has Run Out

This isn't a metaphor. The treasury is LITERALLY EMPTY.

On August 16, 1788, the government announces it can't pay its bills. Payments are suspended. This is effectively a partial bankruptcy—the nightmare scenario everyone had been dreading for years.

The announcement specifies that the government will pay some obligations in cash and others in... paper. Government IOUs. Which everyone knows will be worth less than their face value.

Confidence collapses. Credit markets freeze. The whole financial system, already shaky, starts to seize up.

## Brienne Falls

August 25, 1788. Archbishop Loménie de Brienne resigns.

His "predestination for the highest offices" has ended in total humiliation. He'd spent a lifetime maneuvering for power, finally got it, and discovered he had no idea what to do with it. Every scheme backfired. Every clever plan made things worse.

He'll be made a Cardinal as a consolation prize (the Pope isn't happy about giving it to him, but the King insists). He'll hang around on the edges of the Revolution for a while, then die in 1794—some say by suicide, others say by natural causes while under arrest.

## Necker Returns

And who gets called back to save the day? **Jacques Necker**, the Swiss banker who'd been Controller back in the early 1780s, then got pushed out, then wrote a bestselling critique of the royal finances.

The streets of Paris ERUPT in celebration when his return is announced. Crowds gather. Bonfires are lit. There are shouts of "Long live Necker! Long live the King!"

It's almost religious—like a prophet returning from exile to save his people.

Necker isn't actually that good. His previous tenure was mixed at best. His famous transparency was partly spin. His economic ideas were conventional.

But he represents HOPE. He represents something different from the corrupt incompetents who've been running things. And right now, that's enough.

## The States-General Is Official

On August 8, 1788—even before Brienne formally resigned—the government announced that the States-General would be convened for May 1, 1789.

That's it. The decision everyone has been fighting about for a year. It's done.

No more dodging. No more halfway measures. The nation will be consulted. Representatives will be chosen. They'll gather at Versailles and... something will happen.

What, exactly? Nobody knows.

## The Key Questions

The announcement raised more questions than it answered:

**Who gets to vote?** The States-General had last met in 1614. The rules from that era were archaic. Do they still apply?

**How many representatives from each estate?** There are three estates: Clergy, Nobility, and the Third Estate (everyone else). In 1614, each estate had equal representation despite the Third Estate representing 95%+ of the population. Is that fair?

**How do they vote?** By estate (meaning the clergy and nobles can always outvote the commoners)? Or by head count (meaning the Third Estate could potentially dominate)?

These seem like technical procedural questions. They're actually EVERYTHING. The entire future of France depends on how they're answered.

## The Parlement's Mistake

Remember the Parlement of Paris? The brave defenders of liberty who'd demanded the States-General?

They now made a critical error.

When asked about the rules for the States-General, the Parlement declared that it should follow the forms of 1614: three separate estates, voting by estate.

In other words: the commoners stay in their place.

The public reaction was FURIOUS.

Wait—so this was never about liberty? It was about the NOBLES protecting THEMSELVES? The Parlement doesn't want the people to have power any more than the King does?

In one moment, the Parlement destroyed all the goodwill it had built up. The alliance between the bourgeoisie (educated middle class) and the nobility cracked wide open.

## A New Political Landscape

Suddenly the battle lines are redrawn:

**Before**: King vs. Everyone Else (nobles and commoners united against royal tyranny)

**After**: King and Nobles vs. The People (the privileged classes unite to keep the commoners down)

Or at least, that's how many people start to see it.

A lawyer named Sieyès publishes a pamphlet called "What Is the Third Estate?" His answer: EVERYTHING. The Third Estate is the nation. The nobles and clergy are just parasites living off everyone else's work.

This is incendiary stuff. And it's flying off the shelves.

## The Road to May

As 1788 turns to 1789, France is in a fever of political excitement.

Elections are being organized. Pamphlets are being written. Debates are raging in every salon, every café, every marketplace.

The harvest of 1788 had been terrible. Bread prices are soaring. Unemployment is rising. A brutal winter approaches.

Into this volatile mixture, pour representatives from every corner of France, carrying the hopes and grievances of their communities.

What could possibly go wrong?
