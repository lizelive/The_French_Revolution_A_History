# Loménie's Edicts: The Plan Nobody Wanted

The Notables are gone. Archbishop Loménie de Brienne is now running the show. And his brilliant idea? Push through Calonne's reforms anyway.

## The Contradiction

This is kind of hilarious if you think about it:
1. Calonne proposes reforms
2. The Notables attack Calonne for the reforms
3. Brienne undermines Calonne and gets him fired
4. Brienne takes Calonne's job
5. Brienne... tries to implement Calonne's reforms

The only thing that changed was the name on the door.

## The Edicts

Brienne's plan came down to a series of royal edicts:
- Provincial Assemblies (local self-government—sounds progressive!)
- Free trade in grain (good for the economy, sometimes rough on the poor)
- Abolition of the *corvée* (no more forced road labor—everyone cheers!)
- And of course: the **Stamp Tax** and the **Land Tax**

The first few were popular enough. But those taxes? The privileged classes weren't any happier about them just because Brienne was proposing them instead of Calonne.

## The Parlement Problem

Here's where it gets complicated.

In France, royal edicts didn't become law until the Parlement of Paris "registered" them—basically, confirmed they were valid. Usually this was a formality. The King said "do this," and the Parlement stamped it approved.

But the Parlement could also refuse to register. They could "remonstrate"—send the edict back with objections. The King could override them with a *lit de justice* (literally appearing in person and ordering them to register), but that was a nuclear option that made everyone look bad.

The Parlement of Paris was full of judges who had BOUGHT their positions. They were nobles of the robe, not noble enough for Versailles but plenty noble enough to resent being pushed around. And they'd been reading Rousseau too.

They saw an opportunity.

## The Clash Begins

When Brienne's tax edicts arrived at the Parlement for registration, the judges balked.

"These taxes," they declared, "are unprecedented. We cannot approve them."

What's more, they played the democracy card: "Only the NATION has the right to approve new taxes. And the Nation means the States-General."

Oh snap.

The States-General was France's ancient representative assembly—hadn't met since 1614. Calling for it was revolutionary. It meant admitting that the King couldn't just do whatever he wanted. It meant letting the people have a say.

The judges probably didn't actually WANT democracy. They wanted to block taxes and make themselves look like heroes. But they'd opened Pandora's box.

## The Stakes

What made this crisis different from previous Parlement-vs-King fights?

1. **Public opinion was watching.** Newspapers, pamphlets, gossip—everyone was following the drama.

2. **The government was broke.** Usually the King could outlast the Parlement. Not this time. Every week without the new taxes made the crisis worse.

3. **The precedent of America.** The American Revolution had succeeded. The old idea that kings ruled by divine right was looking shaky.

4. **Enlightenment ideas had spread.** The judges used Rousseau's language about "the nation" and "the general will." This wasn't just a legal dispute—it was ideological.

## Brienne's Dilemma

Brienne was stuck. He couldn't back down without losing all authority. He couldn't push through without a fight. And every day that passed, the treasury got emptier and the opposition got louder.

He decided to try a *lit de justice*—have the King personally command the Parlement to register the edicts.

This would work... temporarily.

But it would also set up the next, even more dramatic confrontation.

Get your popcorn ready.
