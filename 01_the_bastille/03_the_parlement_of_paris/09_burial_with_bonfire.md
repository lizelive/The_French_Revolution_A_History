# Burial with Bonfire: The Old World Burns

The old regime is dying. And as it dies, the people of France hold a wake—complete with bonfires, riots, and a preview of the chaos to come.

## The Winter of 1788-89

This was one of the worst winters in recorded history.

The Seine froze solid. People froze in their homes. The price of bread—already high from a bad harvest—went through the roof. In Paris and elsewhere, the poor were literally starving.

And into this misery, political excitement poured like gasoline on a fire.

## The Pamphlet Wars

Everyone with a pen was writing. The question "What should the States-General do?" generated THOUSANDS of pamphlets, broadsides, and manifestos.

The most famous was Sieyès's "What Is the Third Estate?" but there were many others:
- Arguments for and against voting by head
- Plans to reform the monarchy
- Plans to abolish it entirely
- Economic proposals, constitutional schemes, philosophical treatises

France had never seen anything like this explosion of political writing. The Enlightenment, which had been a salon entertainment for decades, suddenly became practical politics.

## The Elections

Throughout the spring of 1789, elections took place across France.

The system was complex:
- The First Estate (Clergy): Chose representatives through ecclesiastical assemblies
- The Second Estate (Nobility): Chose representatives through regional noble assemblies
- The Third Estate: A multi-tiered system where localities chose electors, who chose more electors, who finally chose representatives

Anyone who paid taxes could participate in the Third Estate elections. In a nation where most people had never had any political voice at all, this was revolutionary.

Local assemblies also drew up *cahiers de doléances*—"books of grievances"—listing their communities' complaints and demands. These cahiers are an incredible historical document: thousands of them survive, painting a detailed picture of what ordinary French people actually wanted.

What did they want? Lower taxes. Fair justice. An end to arbitrary imprisonment. Freedom from feudal dues. Better roads. Some local self-government. Basic dignity.

Mostly, they wanted someone to LISTEN.

## The Réveillon Riot

On April 27-28, 1789, a preview of coming attractions erupted in Paris.

Jean-Baptiste Réveillon ran a wallpaper factory in the Faubourg Saint-Antoine (the same neighborhood where the Bastille stood). He'd allegedly said something about lowering wages—probably taken out of context, but in the current atmosphere, it was enough.

A mob attacked his factory. Then his house. Then anything associated with him.

The army was called in. They fired on the crowd. Hundreds were killed or wounded—the numbers are unclear, but it was the bloodiest day Paris had seen in a long time.

The Réveillon riot wasn't really about Réveillon. It was about everything: hunger, anger, fear, hope, frustration. Years of accumulated grievances exploding in violence.

If this was what happened over a rumor about wages, what would happen when real issues were on the table?

## The Representatives Arrive

Through April and early May, the elected deputies streamed toward Versailles.

They came from every corner of France, representing every shade of opinion:
- Parish priests alongside bishops
- Liberal nobles alongside reactionaries  
- Lawyers, merchants, writers, doctors, farmers
- Some eager to reform, some terrified of change, some with no idea what they wanted

The Third Estate had about 600 deputies—double the clergy and nobility combined. This was a huge victory: King Louis, at Necker's urging, had agreed to "double the Third." But would they get to vote by head? That question remained unanswered.

## May 4, 1789: The Procession

On the day before the States-General officially opened, there was a grand religious procession through Versailles.

The King, the Queen, all the great nobles, the clergy in their vestments, and the deputies of all three estates walked through the streets to attend mass at the Church of Saint-Louis.

The First and Second Estates wore their traditional finery—silks and laces and swords. The Third Estate, by protocol, wore plain black. Side by side, they marched through streets lined with cheering crowds.

It was magnificent. It was medieval. It was the last time the old order would present itself whole and intact.

The next day, the doors of the Salle des Menus-Plaisirs would open, and a new era would begin.

What emerged from that hall would bury the old regime forever.

With bonfires.
