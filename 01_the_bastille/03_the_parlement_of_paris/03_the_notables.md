# The Notables: When the Rich Get Asked to Tax Themselves

February 22, 1787. One hundred and forty-four Very Important People have gathered at Versailles. They're called "Notables"—princes, dukes, archbishops, judges, military leaders. The cream of French society.

Their task: Fix the country's finances.

Their problem: The solution involves them paying taxes.

This is going to go GREAT.

## The Setup

The Assembly of Notables hadn't been convened since 1626—over 160 years. Calling one was basically admitting that normal government had failed. But Calonne was desperate.

Among the attendees:
- Seven Princes of the Blood (including the Duke of Orléans, formerly the Duke of Chartres—yes, the one with the ruined health and the cut-down chestnut trees)
- Top judges from the Parlements
- Military aristocrats
- High clergy

Also lurking around: **Count Mirabeau**, who had rushed back from Berlin hoping to be named Secretary to the Assembly. He got passed over for someone with a better reputation. Mirabeau took it badly, started writing pamphlets attacking everything in sight, and had to flee France again to avoid his SEVENTEENTH *lettre de cachet*.

(This man is exhausting. He'll be back.)

## Calonne's Pitch

Calonne gave the opening speech—and by all accounts, it was BRILLIANT. Lucid, eloquent, comprehensive. He laid out the whole problem: the deficit is enormous, the current taxes don't work, we need reform.

His proposals? Basically Turgot's old plan:
- Provincial assemblies (more local self-government)
- Abolish the *corvée* (forced peasant labor on roads)
- And the big one: A **Land Tax** that everyone pays—including nobles and clergy

This last one was the kicker. For centuries, the privileged classes had been COLLECTING taxes, not PAYING them. Now Calonne was asking them to open their own wallets.

## The Response

You can imagine how well this went.

The Notables were outraged. Their whole identity was based on being exempt from common burdens! Tax the nobles? Tax the clergy? UNTHINKABLE!

But they couldn't just say "we don't want to pay." So they attacked the messenger. The deficit must be Calonne's fault! He's been mismanaging things! What about all that money he spent on the court? Show us the receipts!

Lafayette (yes, the American Revolution hero) went further: he accused Calonne of actual corruption. Others piled on. The Assembly turned into an inquisition.

One day, Calonne stood alone in a meeting room facing all 137 deputies firing questions at him for FIVE SOLID HOURS. By all accounts, he handled it magnificently—answering everything with calm, charm, and precision. But charm wasn't going to cut it. The Notables weren't going to accept any plan that made them pay taxes.

## The Cartoon That Said Everything

Paris was watching. A popular political cartoon showed a farmer addressing his chickens and ducks:

> "Dear animals, I have assembled you to advise me what sauce I shall dress you with."
> 
> A rooster responds: "We don't want to be eaten!"
> 
> The farmer: "You wander from the point."

That was the Notables' situation perfectly captured. They'd been called to approve their own taxation. Their opinions on whether they SHOULD be taxed were irrelevant.

## The Fall of Calonne

Behind the scenes, intrigue was swirling. Archbishop Loménie de Brienne of Toulouse had his eye on Calonne's job. He had allies at court, including the Queen's personal reader. The ground was being mined beneath Calonne's feet.

On April 9, 1787 (the Monday after Easter), Calonne was fired.

One observer reports hearing the news while riding on the boulevards. A moment later, the Duke of Orléans came galloping past "head to the wind" and confirmed it. The magician was done.

Calonne would spend the rest of his life in exile, writing pamphlets nobody read, eventually marrying a rich widow, and dying forgotten. His one talent—making problems disappear through borrowing—had finally reached its limit.

## Enter Loménie de Brienne

The new Controller-General was the same Archbishop who'd been undermining Calonne in the Notables. Loménie de Brienne had spent fifty years maneuvering toward power. Now he finally had it.

Problem: He had NO IDEA what to do with it.

His entire career had been about getting the job. Actually doing the job? He'd never thought that far ahead. Looking inside himself for qualifications, he found... nothing. "Vacuity and possibility."

His solution? Adopt Calonne's plan! The same plan the Notables had rejected! The same plan that got Calonne fired!

Politics, ladies and gentlemen.

## Wrapping It Up

The Notables were becoming a problem. Lafayette was making speeches about "liberty of the subject" and—shockingly—suggesting they should convene a **National Assembly**. Not just Notables chosen by the King, but actual representatives chosen by the people.

The Count d'Artois, presiding over Lafayette's bureau, was horrified: "You demand States-General?"

Lafayette: "Yes, Monseigneur. And even better than that."

D'Artois (to the clerks): "Write it down."

That demand was on the record now. It would be fulfilled sooner than anyone expected.

On May 25, 1787, King Louis XVI gave a closing speech. Then Loménie, then Lamoignon, then others—ten speeches total, a symphony of platitudes, drowning the Notables in a "bravura peal" of royal eloquence. 

And then they were dismissed. Go home, everyone. Thanks for coming. We'll take it from here.

They'd accomplished nothing. But they'd set something in motion that couldn't be stopped.

The word "States-General" was in the air now.

And France would never be the same.
