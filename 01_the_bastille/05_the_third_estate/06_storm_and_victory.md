# Storm and Victory: July 14, 1789

**THE BASTILLE HAS FALLEN.**

This is the central moment. The hinge of history. The day that would be celebrated as long as France exists.

Let's put it all together.

## The Full Timeline

**Morning (6-10 AM):**
- Paris wakes after a sleepless night
- Crowds converge on the Invalides
- The armory is seized: 28,000+ muskets captured
- The crowd now has guns but needs powder

**Late Morning (10 AM - 1 PM):**
- Delegations go to the Bastille seeking gunpowder
- Governor de Launay negotiates, stalls
- He pulls back his cannon as a gesture of good faith
- The crowd grows larger and more impatient

**Early Afternoon (~1:30 PM):**
- The outer drawbridge is broken down
- Crowd surges into the outer courtyard
- Shooting begins—the fortress fires on the attackers
- Dozens of Parisians fall dead and wounded
- Full siege begins

**Mid-Afternoon (3-4 PM):**
- Mutinous Gardes Françaises arrive with cannon
- Artillery trained on the fortress gates
- The situation becomes hopeless for the defenders

**Late Afternoon (~5 PM):**
- White flag raised
- De Launay offers to surrender
- Terms agreed: safe conduct for the garrison
- The drawbridges come down
- **THE BASTILLE FALLS**

## Why This Day Matters

It wasn't the military significance—the Bastille was barely defended and nearly empty.

It wasn't even the symbolic significance—though that was huge.

What mattered was what it PROVED:

1. **The people could fight.** Ordinary Parisians, without training or organization, had taken a royal fortress. They weren't helpless.

2. **The army couldn't be trusted.** The Gardes Françaises—supposedly royal troops—had defected. Soldiers wouldn't fire on their own people.

3. **The King's power was hollow.** All those troop movements, all that threatening buildup—and when push came to shove, they couldn't even protect one fortress in the heart of Paris.

The revolution was real. The revolution could win. The revolution WOULD win.

## The Human Cost

About 100 attackers died. These weren't soldiers—they were carpenters and laborers, shop owners and artisans, ordinary people who saw tyranny and decided to fight it.

The most famous was probably Louis Tournay, a cartwright who personally hacked through the drawbridge chains while under fire. He survived and became a revolutionary hero.

Others weren't so lucky. Their names are recorded in memorials: the first martyrs of liberty.

## The Immediate Aftermath

That evening, triumphant crowds paraded through Paris. They carried the keys to the Bastille, the captured flags, the severed heads of de Launay and the provost Flesselles (we'll get to that).

At the Hôtel de Ville, the electors tried to maintain order. They confirmed Lafayette as commander of the new National Guard. They organized patrols. They sent messages to Versailles.

The night of July 14-15 was as sleepless as the one before, but the mood was different. Terror had become triumph. The impossible had happened.

## The Legend

The Storming of the Bastille became THE origin story of modern France.

Every July 14th, France celebrates *la Fête Nationale*—Bastille Day. Fireworks, parades, military displays. It's the French equivalent of July 4th.

The reality was messier than the legend. The attackers weren't a unified revolutionary force—they were a chaotic mob. The fortress wasn't a hive of oppression—it was nearly empty. The victory wasn't clean—it ended in lynching and severed heads.

But legends aren't about messy details. The legend says: On July 14, 1789, the French people rose up, stormed the fortress of tyranny, and won their freedom.

And in the ways that matter most, that legend is true.
