# Give Us Arms! The Siege Escalates

The afternoon of July 14, 1789. The Bastille is under attack. Blood has been shed. There's no going back now.

## The Siege Intensifies

From about 1:30 PM, the scene around the Bastille was chaos incarnate.

The garrison's musket fire killed and wounded dozens of attackers. The thick walls made any direct assault suicidal. The insurgents shot back, but their fire was ineffective against stone towers.

Smoke filled the air. The clamor was deafening. People ran in all directions—some attacking, some retreating, some bringing ammunition, some carrying wounded.

The crowd had numbers, fury, and muskets. But they lacked artillery, organization, and military expertise.

## The Game-Changers Arrive

Around 3:00 PM, everything changed.

A detachment of **Gardes Françaises**—actual professional soldiers, from the Paris garrison—arrived with cannon.

These weren't untrained civilians. These were military men who had DEFECTED to the revolution. They knew how to use artillery. They knew how to conduct a siege.

They set up their cannon facing the fortress gates. Now the Bastille was in real trouble.

## Inside the Fortress

De Launay was losing his nerve. Or maybe he was just being realistic.

His garrison was divided—the invalides didn't want to fight fellow Frenchmen, while the Swiss were willing but outnumbered. His ammunition would run out. His food was already gone. And now the enemy had cannon pointed at his gates.

He reportedly threatened to blow up the powder magazine—and the whole Bastille with it—rather than surrender. His own men talked him down. They had no desire to die for a lost cause.

## The Surrender

Around 5:00 PM, after about four hours of fighting, a white flag appeared.

De Launay sent a message: He would surrender if his garrison was guaranteed safety.

The crowd agreed. Safe conduct was promised.

The drawbridges were lowered.

The Bastille had fallen.

## The Death Toll

On the attackers' side: about 100 dead, 73 wounded. These were the revolution's first martyrs, ordinary Parisians who died storming a fortress of tyranny.

On the defenders' side: one invalide killed during the siege. Six more would be murdered after the surrender.

De Launay himself... well, we'll get to that.

## What They Found Inside

The crowd poured through the fortress, expecting to find dungeons full of political prisoners, torture chambers, maybe even an Iron Mask.

The actual prisoners: seven men. Four forgers. Two mentally ill. One nobleman imprisoned for incest at his family's request.

Not exactly a roster of martyrs to liberty.

But the symbolism didn't need real victims. The Bastille represented tyranny. The Bastille had fallen. That was enough.

## The Keys

The keys to the Bastille—massive iron things—were seized as trophies.

Within days, they were on their way to America, a gift to George Washington from Lafayette. The keys of oppression, sent to the leader of liberty.

(They're still there, at Mount Vernon.)

## The Demolition Begins

Almost immediately, entrepreneurs started dismantling the fortress. They would sell chunks of stone as souvenirs, carve tiny Bastilles from the rock, make a fortune off revolution merchandise.

Within a year, the Bastille would be completely gone—razed to the ground, leaving only an empty plaza where France would eventually celebrate its most important national holiday.

But first, there was the matter of what happened to the governor.
