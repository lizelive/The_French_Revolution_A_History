# To Arms! The Morning of July 14

July 14, 1789. The date that will define France forever. But first, the chaotic morning that led there.

## The Sleepless City

Paris didn't sleep the night of July 13-14. The tocsin bells rang continuously. Crowds filled the streets. The newly-formed National Guard was trying to organize itself, but it had a problem:

NO WEAPONS.

What good is a militia without muskets? The revolution could be over by tomorrow if the royal troops decided to attack.

Everyone knew there were weapons stockpiled somewhere. The question was where.

## The Hotel des Invalides

Early on the morning of July 14, word spread: The Invalides—the military veterans' hospital—had a huge cache of muskets in its basement.

The crowd converged. Tens of thousands of Parisians—maybe a hundred thousand—flooded toward the Invalides, demanding arms.

The governor, M. de Sombreuil, tried to negotiate. He sent messengers to Versailles asking for instructions. He pleaded for time.

The crowd was having none of it.

By mid-morning, they'd scaled the walls, overwhelmed the token resistance, and flooded into the basements. What they found: **28,000 to 32,000 muskets**. Plus cannons.

Paris now had guns. But guns need powder and shot.

And everybody knew where THAT was kept.

## The Target Emerges

The **Bastille**.

An ancient medieval fortress on the eastern edge of Paris. Originally built to defend the city, it had been converted into a state prison centuries ago. Its high walls and eight towers dominated the neighborhood.

The Bastille wasn't particularly full—only seven prisoners at this point, none of them political heroes. But it had enormous symbolic weight: it represented royal tyranny, arbitrary imprisonment, *lettres de cachet*, the whole apparatus of despotic power.

More importantly, it was rumored to hold huge stores of gunpowder.

Whether the crowd went to the Bastille for the powder, for the symbolism, or just because it was there and they were angry—historians still debate. Probably all three.

By late morning, the tide of armed, furious Parisians was moving toward the fortress.

## The Garrison

The Bastille's governor was Bernard-René de Launay, a career soldier in an impossible position.

His garrison: about 82 *invalides* (retired or wounded soldiers) and 32 Swiss mercenaries. Not enough to hold against a determined assault, but enough to cause terrible casualties if they chose to fight.

His walls were thick—up to nine feet in places. His towers were high. He had cannon positioned in the embrasures.

But he only had one day's food supply. And no clear orders. And thirty thousand angry Parisians outside his gates.

## The Morning Negotiations

Early delegations from the Hôtel de Ville came to negotiate. They wanted the cannons removed from their threatening positions. They wanted assurances the fortress wouldn't fire on the people.

De Launay was conciliatory. He ordered the cannons pulled back from their embrasures. He invited delegates in for breakfast. He promised not to fire unless attacked.

But the crowd outside was growing. Angrier. More impatient.

More delegations arrived. More discussions. De Launay kept dithering. He couldn't surrender without orders—that would be treason. He couldn't resist effectively—he didn't have the men. He was trapped.

## The Point of No Return

Around 1:00 PM, a group of men managed to break into the outer courtyard, and cut the drawbridge chains.

The outer drawbridge crashed down.

The crowd surged forward into the outer court.

Then—gunfire.

The invalides in the towers, seeing an attacking mob, opened fire. De Launay himself may or may not have ordered it. Some accounts say he pulled up the inner drawbridge, trapping the crowd in a killzone.

Whatever the sequence, people were dying. The blood was flowing.

And now it was war.
