# Inertia: The Standoff Begins

May 5, 1789. The States-General officially opens. And immediately, everything grinds to a halt.

## Opening Day

The Salle des Menus-Plaisirs was a converted warehouse, hastily fitted out for this historic occasion. Nearly 1,200 deputies packed into the hall. The King sat on his throne at one end. The three estates were seated in their assigned sections—Third Estate notably farther from the royal presence.

Louis XVI gave an opening speech. It was... fine. He mentioned the financial crisis. He expressed hope for cooperation. He said nothing about voting procedures or constitutional reform.

Necker spoke for three hours. THREE HOURS. His voice gave out and someone else had to read the rest. It was mostly numbers—deficit this, revenue that. Important stuff, but hardly inspiring.

The Third Estate deputies sat there in their plain black, waiting for the real issue to be addressed: Would they vote by head or by order?

It wasn't addressed.

## The Verification Crisis

The first order of business was supposed to be verifying credentials—confirming that each deputy was legitimately elected.

Simple enough, right?

Wrong.

The privileged orders wanted to verify separately. Each estate would check its own credentials, meeting in its own hall. This would establish from day one that the three orders were SEPARATE.

The Third Estate wanted joint verification. All three orders meeting together, checking everyone's credentials as a single body. This would establish from day one that they were ONE assembly.

See the problem?

The procedure itself was the battle. Whoever won on verification would set the precedent for everything else.

## The Stalemate

Day after day, the Third Estate deputies sat in the main hall, waiting.

The nobles met in their chamber and verified their credentials.
The clergy met in their chamber and... debated, actually. Many parish priests wanted to join the Third Estate. The bishops pushed back.

The Third Estate did nothing. They refused to verify themselves separately. They refused to constitute themselves as a separate order. They just... sat there.

It was a brilliant tactical move. By doing nothing, they put the pressure on the other orders. Either the nobles and clergy would have to come join them, or the whole States-General would be paralyzed.

## Week After Week

Days turned into weeks. The Third Estate waited. Delegations went back and forth. Conferences were held. Compromises were proposed and rejected.

Outside, France grew more impatient. Pamphlets flew. Crowds gathered. The bread shortage continued. People were expecting action—reform, relief, SOMETHING. Instead, they got parliamentary deadlock.

Inside the hall, frustration mounted. The Third Estate deputies had come to change France. Instead, they were stuck in procedural limbo, watching the nobles and clergy delay, delay, delay.

## The Anger Builds

Mirabeau, that irrepressible force of nature, made speeches. Passionate, thunderous speeches about the rights of the nation, the sovereignty of the people, the need for the Third Estate to ACT.

Sieyès, cooler but equally determined, argued that the Third Estate should simply declare itself the National Assembly—with or without the other orders.

More moderate deputies urged patience. Keep negotiating. Find a compromise. Don't provoke a crisis.

But the crisis was already there. It had been there since the start. The question was just how it would resolve.

## May Becomes June

By June 10, 1789, over a month had passed since the opening ceremony. Nothing had been accomplished. Not a single tax had been approved, not a single reform enacted, not a single grievance addressed.

The deputies from the Third Estate looked at each other. They looked at the empty hall around them—empty because they were the only ones who came to work every day. They looked at the expectations of millions of French people.

Something had to give.

On June 10, Sieyès made his move. He proposed that the Third Estate issue a final invitation to the other orders, and then proceed with or without them.

The countdown had begun.
