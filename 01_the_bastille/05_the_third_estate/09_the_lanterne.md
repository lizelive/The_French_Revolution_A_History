# The Lanterne: Revolutionary Justice, or Just Violence?

There's a part of the July 14 story we haven't told yet. The dark part. The part where the triumphant mob did things that can't be celebrated.

## De Launay's Death

Governor de Launay surrendered the Bastille with a promise of safe conduct. That promise was broken almost immediately.

As he was being escorted to the Hôtel de Ville for trial, the crowd turned on him. They beat him. They stabbed him. Eventually, someone cut off his head with a pocketknife (or a butcher's cleaver, accounts vary).

His head was stuck on a pike and paraded through the streets.

De Launay was a soldier who had dithered when he should have decided, but he wasn't a monster. The crowd killed him anyway.

## The Lanterne

The "lanterne" was a streetlamp near the Place de Grève, outside the Hôtel de Ville. It had a convenient hook for hanging things.

In July 1789, that hook was used to hang human beings.

Jacques de Flesselles, the provost of Paris—basically the royal administrator of the city—was accused of treachery. (He had allegedly promised weapons to the crowd and then delivered empty boxes.) As he was being taken for questioning, someone shot him. His head joined de Launay's on a pike.

In the following days, more people were lynched at the lanterne: suspected spies, accused hoarders, anyone the crowd decided was an "enemy of the people."

The phrase *À la lanterne!* ("To the lantern!") became a revolutionary slogan—a demand for instant mob justice.

## The Question of Revolutionary Violence

This is the uncomfortable part of the story. The part that doesn't fit neatly into narratives of liberty versus tyranny.

The revolution was made by violence. Not just the controlled violence of soldiers and organized militia, but the wild violence of angry crowds. Heads on pikes. Bodies in the streets. Lynchings and massacres.

Can you have a revolution without this? History suggests: probably not. The old order doesn't surrender without a fight, and fights get ugly.

But does that justify what happened? Can you murder prisoners and call it justice? Can you celebrate liberty while sticking human heads on pikes?

Different people answer that differently.

## The Pattern Is Set

July 1789 established a pattern that would repeat throughout the Revolution:

1. Crisis builds
2. Violence erupts
3. Victories are won
4. Scores are settled (often brutally)
5. Order is restored (temporarily)
6. New crisis builds

Each cycle would be more extreme than the last. What started with severed heads in July 1789 would escalate to mass executions by 1793-94.

The lanterne was the preview. The guillotine was the main feature.

## The Lesson Nobody Learned

Many revolutionaries were uncomfortable with the popular violence. Lafayette tried to establish discipline in the National Guard. The Assembly passed laws against lynching. Moderate deputies gave speeches about the rule of law.

But they also couldn't—or wouldn't—punish the perpetrators. Killing de Launay was technically murder, but nobody was prosecuted. The lanterne victims had no justice.

The message was clear: if the crowd decided you were an enemy of the revolution, there was no law that could protect you.

That's a dangerous precedent to set. And it would come back to haunt many of the people who set it.

## Conclusion

The Bastille fell on July 14, 1789. France celebrates that day every year.

But July 14 wasn't just about liberty and hope. It was also about rage and blood. The Revolution was born in violence, and violence would mark every stage of its development.

Whether that violence was necessary, justified, or simply inevitable—that's the question that hangs over the entire Revolutionary period.

There are no easy answers. There never are.

But the story continues. The revolution had only just begun.
