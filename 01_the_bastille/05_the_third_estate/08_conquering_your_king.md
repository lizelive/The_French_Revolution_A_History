# Conquering Your King: The New Order Emerges

July-August 1789. The revolution has won its first battle. Now comes the harder part: figuring out what to do with victory.

## The Constitutional Project

The National Assembly got to work. Their mandate, as they understood it: write a constitution for France.

But first, they needed to clear away the undergrowth of the old regime. The *cahiers de doléances* had made clear what the people wanted abolished: feudal dues, seigneurial courts, aristocratic privileges, the whole creaky apparatus of medieval hierarchy.

On the night of August 4, 1789, it all came crashing down.

## The Night of August 4

What happened that night was either a planned stroke of political theater or a spontaneous outburst of revolutionary enthusiasm. Probably both.

One noble stood up and renounced his feudal privileges. Then another. Then another. Deputies tried to outdo each other in what they would give up.

- Hunting rights? Gone!
- Private law courts? Abolished!
- Tax exemptions? Surrendered!
- Titles? Well... modified!
- Tithes to the Church? Ended!

By dawn, the entire feudal system had been voted out of existence. A thousand years of hierarchy, swept away in one sleepless night.

## The Declaration of the Rights of Man

On August 26, 1789, the Assembly adopted the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen**.

Its famous opening:

> "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights."

Sixteen more articles followed, establishing the principles that would define modern liberal democracy:
- Natural rights (liberty, property, security, resistance to oppression)
- Popular sovereignty (all authority derives from the nation)
- Equality before the law
- Freedom of speech and religion
- Protection against arbitrary arrest
- Due process

It was the French version of the American Declaration of Independence and Bill of Rights combined. A statement of first principles for a new order.

## The King Problem

All this was great, but there was still a king sitting in Versailles. What to do with him?

The Assembly was trying to create a constitutional monarchy—a king with limited powers, bound by law, sharing authority with an elected legislature. Something like England.

But Louis XVI wasn't enthusiastic. He refused to formally approve either the August 4 decrees or the Declaration of Rights.

He didn't reject them outright—that would provoke another crisis. He just... didn't sign. He stalled. He made vague noises about needing more time to consider.

Sound familiar?

## The Bread Problem

Meanwhile, Paris was still hungry.

The harvest of 1789 had been better than 1788, but it takes time for new grain to reach the market. Bread was still expensive. Lines at bakeries still stretched around the block.

The political crisis had disrupted normal commerce. Aristocrats who used to employ servants and buy luxury goods were fleeing the country, taking their money with them. Unemployment rose.

Revolutionary enthusiasm doesn't fill empty stomachs.

## The Rumor Mill

In this atmosphere, rumors flourished.

The court was plotting another coup! The King was going to flee and return with foreign armies! The aristocrats were hoarding grain to starve the people into submission!

Some rumors were false. Some were exaggerated. Some were completely true. The problem was nobody knew which was which.

Paranoia fed on itself. Every delay, every hesitation, every piece of bad news became evidence of conspiracy.

The King wouldn't sign the Declaration? He must be planning treachery!

Troops were maneuvering near Versailles? Another July 14 was being prepared!

Bread prices were high? The aristocrats were sabotaging the food supply!

## The Powder Keg

By September 1789, the situation was unstable.

The old regime had fallen, but the new one wasn't yet built. The King existed in a twilight zone—officially still ruling, but really just waiting to see what the Assembly would allow. The Assembly had grand principles but limited practical power to implement them. The people had expectations that couldn't be met quickly.

Something was going to snap.

In early October, it did.

A banquet at Versailles. A rumor about cockades. And an army of women marching from Paris.

The revolution was about to take another violent turn.
