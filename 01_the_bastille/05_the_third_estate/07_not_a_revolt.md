# Not a Revolt: The Famous Exchange

The night of July 14, 1789. The Bastille has fallen. The question is: what happens now?

## The News Reaches Versailles

Late in the evening of July 14, the Duke de La Rochefoucauld-Liancourt rushed into the King's bedchamber.

Louis XVI had already gone to bed. He'd spent the day hunting (yes, really—while Paris burned, Louis went hunting; he wrote "nothing" in his diary for July 14, referring to the lack of game). He knew there was trouble in Paris, but he didn't grasp the scale.

Liancourt woke him up and delivered the news: The Bastille had fallen. The garrison was dead or captured. The city was in the hands of the insurgents.

Louis XVI, groggy and confused, reportedly asked: "Is it a revolt?"

Liancourt's response: **"No, Sire. It is a revolution."**

*Non, Sire, c'est une révolution.*

## The Meaning of That Exchange

Liancourt understood what Louis didn't: this wasn't a riot to be suppressed, a protest to be accommodated. This was a fundamental break. The old rules no longer applied.

A revolt can be crushed. A revolution rewrites the game.

Louis XVI would spend the next four years trying to treat the Revolution as a revolt—something that could be managed, negotiated, perhaps eventually reversed. He never fully accepted that the world had changed.

## The Morning of July 15

Louis XVI did something he'd never done before: he went to the National Assembly without ceremony, without troops, almost as a supplicant.

He announced that he was withdrawing the troops from Paris. He was trusting himself to the Assembly and to his good people.

The deputies cheered wildly. Some wept with joy. They escorted the King back to the palace in triumph.

For a brief, shining moment, it looked like reconciliation was possible. A reforming monarch, a grateful nation, a bloodless constitutional settlement.

## The Decision to Go to Paris

But Paris wasn't satisfied with a speech in Versailles. The city demanded the King himself.

On July 17, Louis XVI went to Paris. No guards except a citizen escort. No weapons except his good intentions.

The streets were lined with National Guard militia—the same armed citizens who had stormed the Bastille three days earlier. Louis had to trust that they wouldn't tear him apart.

They didn't. But they also made clear who was in charge now.

## The Cockade Moment

At the Hôtel de Ville, the new revolutionary authorities presented Louis with a tricolor cockade—the red and blue of Paris, bracketing the white of the monarchy.

Louis pinned it to his hat. The crowd roared approval.

In that moment, the absolute monarch of France submitted to the symbol of the revolution. He was no longer Louis By the Grace of God, whose will was law. He was Louis, constitutional king of a sovereign nation.

It was done with ceremony and apparent goodwill. But it was done.

## What the Hardliners Did

While Louis was making nice with Paris, the hardliners were fleeing.

The Count d'Artois left France that very night—July 17, 1789—beginning an exile that would last 25 years. He was the first of the *émigrés*, the aristocrats who fled the revolution and would later return with foreign armies.

Others followed: princes of the blood, conservative nobles, anyone who saw which way the wind was blowing and didn't like it.

The court party was crumbling. The revolution was consolidating.

## What Came Next

Louis XVI returned to Versailles, his prestige shaken but apparently intact.

Necker was recalled in triumph. The hardline ministers were dismissed.

The National Assembly got down to the business of writing a constitution.

Everything seemed to be settling down.

But severed heads were still parading through the streets of Paris. The violence had been released and couldn't easily be put back. The King had bent, but had he broken? The court had retreated, but had it surrendered?

These questions would dominate the next four years, until they were finally answered on a January morning in 1793.

But that's getting ahead of ourselves.

For now: the Bastille had fallen. The King had bowed. The revolution had won.

What would it do with its victory?
