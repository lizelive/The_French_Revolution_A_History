# The Procession: The Last Parade of the Old World

May 4, 1789. The grand opening ceremony of the States-General begins with a religious procession through the streets of Versailles. It's magnificent. It's medieval. It's the last time the Old Regime will present itself whole and intact.

## The Scene

Tens of thousands of spectators line the streets. Every window is occupied. People have climbed onto rooftops for a better view.

The procession begins at the Church of Notre-Dame and winds through Versailles to the Church of Saint-Louis, where a mass will be celebrated. All 1,200 deputies participate, along with the entire royal court.

## The Costumes Tell the Story

The First Estate (Clergy) marches in ecclesiastical splendor—bishops in purple and gold, abbots in black silk, parish priests in simple cassocks. The contrast is stark between the princes of the church and the humble curés.

The Second Estate (Nobility) wears its finest: silk coats embroidered with gold, swords at their sides, plumed hats. They look like peacocks, and they know it.

The Third Estate? Plain black. By protocol, the commoners must wear simple dark clothing. No embroidery. No swords. No plumes.

The visual message is unmistakable: HERE is the glory and power of France (the first two estates). And here, in black, are the commoners—respectable, certainly, but subordinate.

Some observers noticed something else: there were MORE deputies in black than in all the colored finery combined. The sea of dark coats outnumbered the silk and gold. The silent majority.

## The Royal Family

The King and Queen appeared in full majesty. Louis XVI carried himself well—he could look kingly when he had to. Marie Antoinette was beautiful but subdued (her son was dying in a back room of the palace).

The crowd's response was... telling.

When the King passed, there were cheers. "Long live the King!" The people still loved their monarch, or at least the IDEA of monarchy.

When the Queen passed, there was silence. Or worse—murmurs. A few hisses. Marie Antoinette noticed. She went pale.

Her reputation, destroyed by scandal and propaganda, could not be rebuilt by one procession. The Austrian woman. The Madame Deficit. The people remembered.

## The Duke of Orléans

The Duke of Orléans, marching with the nobility, made a point of leaving his proper place and walking among the Third Estate deputies instead.

The crowd went wild. "Long live the Duke of Orléans! Long live our father!"

Was it a calculated political move? Genuine sympathy for the commons? A middle finger to his royal cousins? Probably all three.

The gesture was noted. By everyone.

## The Mass

At the Church of Saint-Louis, the Bishop of Nancy preached the sermon.

He chose his text carefully: "Behold, the day of our redemption draweth nigh."

The Third Estate deputies heard it as a promise. The nobles heard it as a warning.

The Bishop praised the King for summoning the nation together. He spoke of reform, renewal, reconciliation. He invoked the blessings of Providence on this great undertaking.

It was a good sermon. It meant nothing. By the time those deputies left that church, the forces in motion could not be stopped by prayers.

## What Nobody Said

Throughout the procession and the mass, one word hung in the air, unspoken:

**Constitution.**

Would there be one? Who would write it? What would it say?

The deputies knew. The crowds knew. Even the King knew, in his uncertain way.

This wasn't just about taxes anymore. It wasn't just about voting procedures. France was on the verge of remaking itself from the ground up.

Tomorrow, in the Salle des Menus-Plaisirs, the real work would begin.

## The End of the Beginning

The procession ended. The deputies dispersed to their lodgings. The crowds went home.

The sun set on May 4, 1789—the last day of the old world.

Tomorrow, everything would change.
