# The Election: France Finds Its Voice

January to April 1789. All across France, people are choosing representatives. It's chaotic, exciting, and unprecedented.

## How the Elections Worked

The system was complicated (of course it was):

**First Estate (Clergy)**: About 300 seats. Each diocese held an assembly of all local clergy, who elected deputies. Parish priests vastly outnumbered bishops and abbots—which meant the clerical delegation would be more reform-minded than the Church hierarchy wanted.

**Second Estate (Nobility)**: About 300 seats. Nobles met in regional assemblies and elected their own. Many liberal nobles got chosen, including some who would become revolutionary leaders.

**Third Estate (Everyone Else)**: About 600 seats. The process was multi-tiered:
1. Local assemblies (often at the parish level) chose electors
2. Those electors chose more electors
3. The final electors chose the actual deputies

This meant that urban professionals—lawyers, merchants, doctors—dominated the process. They had time for meetings and knew how to work the system. Peasants mostly just submitted their grievances and went back to the fields.

## The Cahiers de Doléances

Each assembly also drew up a *cahier de doléances*—a notebook of grievances—listing what they wanted the States-General to address.

Thousands of these cahiers survive. They're an incredible snapshot of what ordinary French people actually cared about:

From the peasants:
- End the *gabelle* (salt tax) and other crushing taxes
- Abolish feudal dues and seigneurial rights
- Make the nobles and clergy pay their fair share
- Protect us from arbitrary justice

From the townspeople:
- More say in local government
- Fair courts and equal law
- Freedom of the press
- End the maze of internal tariffs strangling trade

From everyone:
- Regular meetings of the States-General
- A constitution limiting royal power
- Accountability in government spending

What's remarkable is how CONSISTENT the cahiers were across France. The grievances might vary in detail, but the basic demands were the same everywhere: fairness, transparency, reform.

## The Rising Stars

The elections produced a remarkable cohort of Third Estate deputies:

**Mirabeau**: Yes, he's back. Rejected by the Provençal nobility (because of his scandals), he ran as a Third Estate candidate in both Aix and Marseille—and won both. He chose Aix. The enfant terrible of the aristocracy was about to become the voice of the people.

**Robespierre**: A young lawyer from Arras, unknown outside his province. Intense, principled, incorruptible. Nobody had any idea what he would become.

**Barnave, Mounier, Target, Tronchet**: Lawyers, mostly. Men who'd read their Rousseau and Montesquieu, who believed in constitutional government and natural rights.

**Abbé Sieyès**: Elected in Paris. His pamphlet had made him famous; now he'd have a seat at the table.

These men would reshape France—and in some cases, die for it.

## The Growing Tension

As the elections proceeded, tension mounted.

The spring of 1789 was brutal. The terrible harvest of 1788 meant bread was scarce and expensive. Unemployment was rampant. People were literally starving.

Riots broke out. The Réveillon riot in Paris (April 27-28) saw hundreds killed when troops fired on protesters. It was a taste of things to come.

Meanwhile, pamphlets kept flowing. Speeches kept being made. Expectations kept rising.

The Third Estate deputies, in particular, felt the weight of their mandate. The cahiers made clear what the people wanted. Could they deliver?

## What Everyone Expected

- **The Court expected** a manageable gathering that would approve some taxes, argue for a while, and go home. Business as usual.

- **The Nobles expected** to defend their privileges while maybe accepting some minor reforms. They'd done this before.

- **The Third Estate expected**... something more. Something transformative. A new France.

- **The common people expected** relief. An end to their suffering. Someone, finally, to speak for them.

These expectations could not all be met. Someone was going to be disappointed.

## The Countdown

By late April, the deputies were streaming into Versailles. They came from every corner of France—Brittany and Provence, Paris and the Pyrenees, cities and villages.

On May 4, they would process through the streets in the grand opening ceremony.

On May 5, they would take their seats in the Salle des Menus-Plaisirs and hear the King's opening address.

And then? Nobody knew.

France was about to discover what it wanted to be.
